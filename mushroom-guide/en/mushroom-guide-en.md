## Spot, Identify & Stay Safe on the Trail
### By Paola Adventurer | Archery · Camping · Explore

# How to Use This Guide

Hey! If you're reading this, you're probably the kind of person who notices things on the trail that most people walk right past. That weird orange shelf growing on a fallen oak. That white pom-pom hanging from a beech tree. That golden funnel poking out of the moss.

You've been curious. Now you'll know what you're looking at.

**This guide covers 10 mushrooms every hiker should recognize** — 7 edible species and 3 deadly toxic ones. For each one, you'll get:

- What it looks like (cap, gills/pores, stem, size)
- Where and when to find it
- Which regions it grows in
- What it could be confused with
- Whether it's safe or dangerous

**How to search by area:** Jump to the **Regional Quick-Reference** at the end of this guide. Find your region, see which of the 10 mushrooms grow there, and flip to those pages.

**Want to print?** Each mushroom takes up its own section. Print only the ones you need for your area, or print the full guide — your call.

> **IMPORTANT SAFETY NOTICE:** This guide is for educational purposes and general identification awareness. Never eat a wild mushroom based solely on a written guide. When in doubt, leave it alone. If you suspect mushroom poisoning, call Poison Control (1-800-222-1222 in the US) or your local emergency number immediately. Always consult with an experienced forager or mycologist before consuming any wild mushroom.

# Golden Rules Before You Start

Before we get into the mushrooms, burn these five rules into your brain:

1. **When in doubt, throw it out.** No meal is worth a hospital visit. If you're not 100% sure, walk away.
2. **Never eat raw wild mushrooms.** Even the safe ones need to be cooked.
3. **Start with one species at a time.** Don't try to learn all 10 at once. Pick one edible, learn it cold, then move to the next.
4. **Spore prints don't lie.** When visual ID isn't enough, a spore print gives you a second confirmation. Place the cap gill-side down on a piece of paper (half white, half dark) for 4–8 hours. The color of the spore deposit narrows your ID fast.
5. **Photograph everything.** Cap top, underside (gills/pores), stem, base, and the surrounding habitat. If you need help from a local foraging group later, these photos are gold.

# THE EDIBLE 7

## 1. Chanterelle
### *Cantharellus cibarius*

**Status: EDIBLE — Beginner-friendly**

### What it looks like
- **Cap:** Golden to egg-yolk yellow, 2–5 inches wide, wavy and funnel-shaped with irregular edges
- **Underside:** False gills — forked ridges that run down the stem, not true blade-like gills. This is the key ID feature
- **Stem:** Solid, same color as the cap, tapers toward the base
- **Flesh:** White to pale yellow when cut open
- **Smell:** Fruity, almost like apricots
- **Size:** 1–6 inches tall

### Where & when
- **Habitat:** Forest floors near oaks, conifers, and hardwoods. Grows from the ground (never from wood)
- **Season:** Summer through early fall (June–October)
- **Grows:** Scattered or in small groups, rarely in dense clusters

### Regions
Pacific Northwest | Northeast | Southeast | Midwest

### Watch out for: Jack O'Lantern (TOXIC — #10 in this guide)
The Jack O'Lantern is the chanterelle's evil twin. Key differences:
- Jack O'Lanterns have **true, sharp gills** (not forked ridges)
- Jack O'Lanterns grow **in clusters on wood** (chanterelles grow alone from soil)
- Jack O'Lanterns glow faintly **in the dark** (bioluminescent)

### If you find one
Cook thoroughly in butter or oil. Never eat raw. Chanterelles are meaty, peppery, and slightly fruity — one of the best-tasting wild mushrooms in the world.

## 2. Morel
### *Morchella esculenta*

**Status: EDIBLE — Beginner-friendly**

### What it looks like
- **Cap:** Honeycomb-like surface with deep pits and ridges, tan to dark brown, cone-shaped
- **Inside:** Completely hollow from top to bottom when sliced in half — this is the #1 ID feature
- **Stem:** White to cream, also hollow, slightly granular texture
- **Size:** 2–6 inches tall

### Where & when
- **Habitat:** Near dead or dying elms, ash, apple, and tulip poplar trees. Loves burned areas (post-wildfire spots are morel goldmines)
- **Season:** Spring (March–May), one of the first mushrooms of the year
- **Grows:** Alone or in scattered groups

### Regions
Pacific Northwest | Northeast | Southeast | Midwest | Rocky Mountains

### Watch out for: False Morel (*Gyromitra esculenta*)
The false morel can cause serious illness or death. Key differences:
- False morels have **brain-like, wrinkled caps** (not a clean honeycomb pattern)
- False morels are **NOT hollow** inside — they have cottony, chambered flesh
- When in doubt, slice it in half. Hollow = true morel. Chambered = false morel. Walk away.

### If you find one
Soak in salt water for 30 minutes to remove insects. Cook thoroughly — never eat raw. Morels are nutty, earthy, and considered a delicacy.

## 3. Chicken of the Woods
### *Laetiporus sulphureus*

**Status: EDIBLE — Beginner-friendly**

### What it looks like
- **Shape:** Large shelf/bracket fungus growing in overlapping layers on tree trunks or logs
- **Top:** Bright orange to salmon
- **Underside:** Bright sulfur-yellow with tiny pores (no gills)
- **Flesh:** Thick, white, firm when young — gets chalky and tough when old
- **Size:** Individual shelves 2–12 inches wide; entire cluster can be 2+ feet across

### Where & when
- **Habitat:** On dead or living hardwoods, especially oaks. Also found on dead conifers (but avoid those — see note below)
- **Season:** Late spring through fall (May–November)
- **Grows:** In large shelving clusters on wood — never from the ground

### Regions
Pacific Northwest | Northeast | Southeast | Midwest

### Watch out for
- **Avoid specimens growing on conifers, eucalyptus, or yew trees** — they can cause GI distress even though the mushroom itself is the same species
- **Old specimens** turn pale, chalky, and crumbly — only eat young, vibrant ones

### If you find one
Young chicken of the woods has a texture remarkably similar to actual chicken breast when cooked. Slice the tender outer edges (skip the tough base), sauté in oil with garlic, and season. A favorite for trail-to-table meals.

## 4. Hen of the Woods (Maitake)
### *Grifola frondosa*

**Status: EDIBLE — Beginner-friendly**

### What it looks like
- **Shape:** Large rosette of overlapping, fan-shaped caps growing from a single base — looks like a ruffled hen sitting at the base of a tree
- **Color:** Gray-brown on top, white underneath
- **Underside:** Tiny white pores (no gills)
- **Stem:** Short, branching, white, all connected at a central core
- **Size:** Can grow massive — 10 to 50+ pounds in a single cluster

### Where & when
- **Habitat:** Base of living or dead hardwoods, especially oaks. Returns to the same spot year after year
- **Season:** Fall (September–November)
- **Grows:** In large clusters at the base of trees

### Regions
Northeast | Southeast | Midwest

### Watch out for: Black-Staining Polypore (*Meripilus sumstinei*)
Similar look, but:
- **Stains black** when bruised or cut — hen of the woods does not
- Not dangerous, but not as good eating

### If you find one
Maitake is prized in Japanese cuisine. Tear into pieces, sauté until crispy on the edges. Rich, savory, umami-heavy flavor. Dries and freezes well for later use.

## 5. Lion's Mane
### *Hericium erinaceus*

**Status: EDIBLE — Virtually no dangerous look-alikes**

### What it looks like
- **Shape:** A single white globe covered in long, cascading, icicle-like spines — looks like a white pom-pom or a waterfall of tiny teeth
- **Color:** Pure white when young, yellowing with age
- **No cap, no stem, no gills** — completely unique shape
- **Size:** 4–12 inches across

### Where & when
- **Habitat:** On dead or dying hardwoods, especially beech, oak, and maple. Grows high up on standing dead trees or on fallen logs
- **Season:** Late summer through fall (August–November)
- **Grows:** Single specimens on wood

### Regions
Pacific Northwest | Northeast | Southeast | Midwest

### Watch out for
Almost nothing. Lion's mane is one of the safest wild mushrooms to identify because nothing else looks quite like it. Other *Hericium* species (bear's head, coral tooth) are all edible too.

### If you find one
Slice into thick "steaks" and sear in butter until golden on both sides. The texture is like crab or lobster — no joke. Also valued for potential cognitive health benefits.

## 6. Oyster Mushroom
### *Pleurotus ostreatus*

**Status: EDIBLE — Beginner-friendly**

### What it looks like
- **Cap:** Fan or oyster-shell shaped, 2–8 inches wide, smooth
- **Color:** White, cream, gray, or tan (sometimes pale brown)
- **Gills:** White, running down a short stubby stem (or no stem at all)
- **Stem:** Short, off-center, attached to the side of the cap
- **Grows on:** Dead hardwood — logs, stumps, standing dead trees
- **Smell:** Mild, pleasant, slightly like anise
- **Size:** Individual caps 2–8 inches; clusters can cover a log

### Where & when
- **Habitat:** Dead or dying hardwoods. Loves fallen logs in damp forests
- **Season:** Spring and fall primarily, but can fruit year-round in mild climates — even in winter
- **Grows:** In overlapping shelf-like clusters on wood

### Regions
Pacific Northwest | Northeast | Southeast | Midwest | Rocky Mountains

### Watch out for: Angel Wings (*Pleurocybella porrigens*)
- Angel wings are **pure white, paper-thin**, and grow on **conifer** wood (not hardwood)
- Oyster mushrooms are **thicker, fleshier**, and grow on **hardwood**
- Stick to hardwood-growing specimens and you're safe

### If you find one
One of the most versatile wild mushrooms. Sauté, stir-fry, bread and fry, add to soups — they work with almost anything. Mild, slightly sweet flavor.

## 7. Porcini (King Bolete)
### *Boletus edulis*

**Status: EDIBLE — Intermediate ID**

### What it looks like
- **Cap:** Smooth, convex, brown to reddish-brown, 3–10 inches wide, slightly sticky when wet
- **Underside:** Spongy pore surface (NOT gills) — white when young, turning yellow-green with age
- **Stem:** Thick, bulbous, white to tan, with fine white netting (reticulation) on the upper portion
- **Flesh:** White, firm, does NOT change color when cut
- **Size:** 4–10 inches tall, sturdy and hefty

### Where & when
- **Habitat:** Under conifers (spruce, pine, fir) and some hardwoods, in mountainous and northern forests
- **Season:** Summer through fall (July–October)
- **Grows:** Alone or scattered on forest floors

### Regions
Pacific Northwest | Northeast | Midwest | Rocky Mountains

### Watch out for: Bitter Bolete (*Tylopilus felleus*)
- Looks nearly identical but tastes **intensely bitter** — one bite will tell you
- The netting on the stem is **darker/coarser** than porcini's fine white netting
- Pore surface turns **pinkish** with age (porcini stays yellow-green)
- Not toxic, just terrible tasting — will ruin a dish

### If you find one
Porcini is one of the most sought-after culinary mushrooms in the world. Slice and sauté fresh, or dry slices for incredible concentrated flavor in risottos, pastas, and soups. The stem is edible too.

## 8. Death Cap
### *Amanita phalloides*

**Status: DEADLY TOXIC — Responsible for most mushroom-related deaths worldwide**

### What it looks like
- **Cap:** 2–6 inches wide, smooth, greenish-yellow to olive-green (sometimes pale white or brownish), slightly sticky when wet
- **Gills:** White, closely spaced, free (not attached to the stem)
- **Stem:** White, 3–6 inches tall, with a **skirt-like ring** (annulus) near the top
- **Base:** Bulbous, sitting inside a **cup-like sac (volva)** — often buried in soil. DIG IT UP to see this critical feature
- **Spore print:** White
- **Smell:** Young specimens smell pleasant, like honey or roses. This is deceptive.

### Where & when
- **Habitat:** Under oaks and other hardwoods, in parks, yards, and forests. Originally from Europe, now widespread in North America
- **Season:** Fall and winter in mild climates; late summer through fall elsewhere
- **Grows:** Alone or in small groups on the ground

### Regions
Pacific Northwest | Northeast | Southeast (expanding range)

### Why it's so dangerous
- Contains **amatoxins** that destroy the liver and kidneys
- **Symptoms are delayed 6–24 hours** after eating — by the time you feel sick, severe organ damage may already be happening
- There is a deceptive "recovery" phase where you feel better before the final, potentially fatal stage
- **Half a cap can kill an adult**
- There is no reliable home antidote. If you suspect death cap ingestion, go to the ER immediately

### Commonly confused with
- Paddy straw mushrooms (in Asian communities)
- Young puffballs (before the cap opens)
- Button mushrooms (to the untrained eye)

### If you see one
Do not touch and eat. Handling alone is not dangerous — the toxins must be ingested. Take photos for your records and move on.

## 9. Destroying Angel
### *Amanita bisporigera / A. virosa / A. ocreata*

**Status: DEADLY TOXIC — As lethal as the Death Cap**

### What it looks like
- **Cap:** 2–5 inches wide, pure white, smooth, slightly convex to flat
- **Gills:** White, free from the stem
- **Stem:** White, tall and slender, with a **fragile skirt-like ring** that may disappear with age
- **Base:** Bulbous with a **sac-like volva** — often buried underground. DIG IT UP
- **Spore print:** White
- **Entire mushroom:** Completely white. Elegantly proportioned. Looks innocent.

### Where & when
- **Habitat:** Near oaks, pines, and mixed forests. Also appears in lawns and gardens near trees
- **Season:** Summer through fall
- **Grows:** Alone or in small groups on the ground

### Regions
Pacific Northwest | Northeast | Southeast | Midwest

### Why it's so dangerous
- Contains the **same amatoxins** as the Death Cap
- Symptoms, timeline, and lethality are identical to the Death Cap
- Its **pure white, "clean" appearance** makes it look harmless — which is exactly why it's so deadly
- Nicknamed "Destroying Angel" because it looks beautiful and kills without mercy

### Commonly confused with
- Button mushrooms (*Agaricus bisporus*)
- Young puffballs
- Meadow mushrooms
- Any small-to-medium white mushroom

### The #1 rule with white mushrooms
**If it's white, has gills, and grows from the ground — treat it as deadly until proven otherwise.** This single rule could save your life.

## 10. Jack O'Lantern
### *Omphalotus olearius*

**Status: TOXIC — Will make you very sick, rarely fatal**

### What it looks like
- **Cap:** 2–8 inches wide, bright orange to orange-yellow, smooth, convex to funnel-shaped
- **Gills:** True, sharp, blade-like gills (not ridges) — orange, closely spaced, running slightly down the stem. **Gills glow faintly green in complete darkness** (bioluminescent)
- **Stem:** Orange, solid, central, tapers toward the base
- **Grows on:** Wood — stumps, buried roots, base of trees. **Always on or near wood**
- **Growth pattern:** Dense clusters, not solitary

### Where & when
- **Habitat:** At the base of hardwood stumps or from buried roots (may look like it's growing from soil, but there's wood underneath)
- **Season:** Summer through fall (July–November)
- **Grows:** In clusters — multiple mushrooms from the same point

### Regions
Northeast | Southeast | Midwest

### Why it matters
The Jack O'Lantern is the mushroom most commonly confused with the Chanterelle (#1 in this guide). Every year, people get sick making this mistake. Here's how to tell them apart:

| Feature | Chanterelle | Jack O'Lantern |
|---------|------------|----------------|
| Gills | False gills (forked ridges) | True, sharp gills |
| Growth | From soil, scattered | In clusters, on/near wood |
| Color inside | Pale/white flesh | Orange flesh throughout |
| Bioluminescence | No | Yes (gills glow in the dark) |
| Smell | Fruity, apricot-like | Unpleasant |

### If someone eats one
Symptoms (severe cramps, vomiting, diarrhea) usually begin within 1–2 hours. It's extremely unpleasant but rarely fatal in healthy adults. Seek medical attention.

# Emergency: What to Do If Someone Eats a Toxic Mushroom

**Time matters. Act fast.**

1. **Call Poison Control immediately:** 1-800-222-1222 (US) or your local emergency number
2. **Do NOT wait for symptoms.** With Death Cap and Destroying Angel, symptoms are delayed 6–24 hours. By the time you feel sick, organ damage is underway
3. **Save a sample** of the mushroom (or take clear photos) — this helps medical professionals identify what was eaten and choose the right treatment
4. **Do NOT try to induce vomiting** unless specifically told to by Poison Control or a doctor
5. **Go to the emergency room** — do not try to "wait it out" at home
6. **Note the time** the mushroom was eaten and when symptoms appeared

### Symptoms by toxin type

**Amatoxin poisoning (Death Cap, Destroying Angel):**
- 6–24 hours after eating: severe abdominal cramps, vomiting, watery diarrhea
- 24–72 hours: deceptive improvement ("I feel better")
- 3–5 days: liver and kidney failure. This is the critical danger zone

**GI-irritant poisoning (Jack O'Lantern and most other toxic mushrooms):**
- 30 minutes to 2 hours: nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, cramps
- Usually resolves within 24 hours with supportive care
- Still see a doctor — don't self-diagnose the toxin type

# What to Bring: Your Mushroom Hunting Gear List

You don't need much. Here's what fits in a daypack and makes all the difference:

### The Essentials
- **A small knife or pocket blade** — to cleanly cut mushrooms at the base without disturbing the mycelium underground
- **Paper bags or wax paper bags** — never plastic. Plastic traps moisture and turns your finds into mush. Paper lets them breathe
- **A basket or mesh bag** — keeps specimens intact and lets spores drop as you walk (you're literally seeding the forest for next year)
- **Your phone (camera)** — for photos of every find: cap, underside, stem, base, and habitat. Non-negotiable

### Nice to Have
- **A small hand lens / magnifying glass (10x)** — for examining gill structure, pore surfaces, and stem details up close
- **A field notebook or notes app** — jot down what tree species are nearby, the soil type, weather conditions. Your future self will thank you
- **Aluminum foil** — wrap individual specimens to keep them separated and protected
- **White + dark paper (index cards work)** — for spore prints right on the trail. Place the cap gill-side down, cover with a cup, and check after a few hours
- **Wet wipes** — always wash your hands after handling unknown mushrooms, especially before eating trail snacks
- **This guide (printed)** — print the mushroom pages for your region and the Quick Reference. Laminate if you want them to survive rain

### What NOT to Bring
- **Plastic bags** — they destroy specimens. Leave them home
- **Overconfidence** — the most dangerous thing you can carry into the woods. When in doubt, leave it

# Your Trail ID Checklist

Use this checklist every time you find a mushroom on the trail:

- [ ] **Where is it growing?** Ground, wood, tree trunk, buried wood?
- [ ] **What's nearby?** Oak, beech, conifer, mixed forest?
- [ ] **How is it growing?** Alone, scattered, dense cluster?
- [ ] **Cap:** Color, shape, size, texture (smooth, sticky, scaly)?
- [ ] **Underside:** Gills, pores, ridges, or teeth/spines?
- [ ] **Stem:** Color, thickness, ring/skirt, cup at base?
- [ ] **Cut it open:** Hollow? Solid? Does the flesh change color?
- [ ] **Smell:** Fruity? Earthy? Unpleasant? Like nothing?
- [ ] **Photos taken?** Top, underside, stem, base, habitat — at least 5 shots
- [ ] **Spore print started?** White paper + dark paper, gill-side down, 4–8 hours
