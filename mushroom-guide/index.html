Chanterelle
Cantharellus cibarius
EdibleJun–Oct

One of the world's most popular wild edible mushrooms, prized for its golden color, fruity apricot aroma, and peppery flavor.

Key ID: Golden funnel shape, false gills (forked ridges, not blades), fruity apricot smell. Grows from soil, never wood.

What It Looks Like

  • Cap: Golden to egg-yolk yellow, 2–5 in wide, wavy funnel with irregular edges
  • Underside: False gills — forked ridges running down the stem (NOT blade-like)
  • Stem: Solid, same golden color, tapers toward base
  • Flesh: White to pale yellow when cut
  • Smell: Fruity, like apricots

Where & When

Forest floors near oaks, conifers, hardwoods. Scattered or small groups. Summer–early fall.

Confirm It's a Real Chanterelle

It must have blunt forked ridges (not true sharp gills), grow from soil (not in clusters on wood), and the flesh inside must be pale/white (not orange throughout).

How to Cook

Cook thoroughly in butter or oil. Never eat raw. Meaty, peppery, slightly fruity — one of the best wild mushrooms in the world.

Morel
Morchella esculenta
EdibleMar–May

A highly sought-after spring mushroom with a distinctive honeycomb cap. Among the most recognizable wild mushrooms and a favorite of foragers worldwide.

Key ID: Honeycomb cap with deep pits. Completely hollow from top to bottom when sliced. Cone-shaped, tan to dark brown.

What It Looks Like

  • Cap: Honeycomb surface with deep pits and ridges, tan to dark brown, cone-shaped
  • Inside: Completely hollow top to bottom — the #1 ID feature
  • Stem: White to cream, also hollow, slightly granular
  • Size: 2–6 inches tall

Where & When

Near dead/dying elms, ash, apple trees. Post-wildfire areas are goldmines. Spring (Mar–May), one of the first mushrooms of the year.

Confirm It's a Real Morel

Slice it in half: a real Morel is completely hollow inside. If the inside is cottony or has chambers, it is not a Morel — discard it. The cap must have clean honeycomb pits, not brain-like wrinkles.

How to Cook

Soak in salt water 30 min to remove insects. Cook thoroughly. Nutty, earthy, considered a delicacy.

Chicken of the Woods
Laetiporus sulphureus
EdibleMay–Nov

A bright orange shelf fungus that tastes remarkably like chicken when cooked. One of the easiest wild mushrooms to identify thanks to its unmistakable color and growth pattern.

Key ID: Bright orange/yellow shelf fungus in overlapping layers on tree trunks. Sulfur-yellow underside with tiny pores.

What It Looks Like

  • Shape: Large shelf/bracket in overlapping layers on trunks or logs
  • Top: Bright orange to salmon
  • Underside: Sulfur-yellow, tiny pores (no gills)
  • Flesh: Thick, white, firm when young — chalky when old

Where & When

On dead or living hardwoods, especially oaks. Late spring–fall. Always on wood, never from ground.

Important Notes

Avoid specimens on conifers, eucalyptus, or yew — can cause GI distress. Only eat young, vibrant ones.

How to Cook

Texture like actual chicken breast. Slice tender outer edges, sauté with garlic.

Hen of the Woods (Maitake)
Grifola frondosa
EdibleSep–Nov

A large, gray-brown mushroom that grows in ruffled clusters at the base of oak trees. Known as "Maitake" in Japanese, valued for both culinary and medicinal uses.

Key ID: Large rosette of gray-brown overlapping fans at tree bases (especially oaks). White pores underneath. Can grow 10–50+ lbs.

What It Looks Like

  • Shape: Ruffled rosette of overlapping fan-shaped caps from one base
  • Color: Gray-brown on top, white underneath
  • Underside: Tiny white pores (no gills)
  • Returns to the same spot year after year

Where & When

Base of living or dead hardwoods, especially oaks. Fall (Sep–Nov).

Confirm It's Hen of the Woods

Press or cut the flesh — it should not stain black. If it turns black, it's a different species. Discard it.

How to Cook

Tear into pieces, sauté until crispy on edges. Rich umami flavor.

Lion's Mane
Hericium erinaceus
EdibleAug–Nov

A unique white mushroom that hangs from hardwood trees like a shaggy pom-pom. One of the safest wild mushrooms to identify — nothing else looks like it.

Key ID: White pom-pom with cascading icicle-like spines. No cap, no stem, no gills. Virtually no dangerous look-alikes.

What It Looks Like

  • Shape: Single white globe with long cascading spines
  • Color: Pure white when young, yellowing with age
  • Unique: No cap, no stem, no gills — nothing else looks like it

Where & When

On dead/dying hardwoods (beech, oak, maple). Late summer–fall.

How to Cook

Slice into thick "steaks" and sear in butter. Texture like crab or lobster.

Oyster Mushroom
Pleurotus ostreatus
EdibleYear-round

One of the most common and versatile wild mushrooms, growing on dead hardwood year-round. A great beginner mushroom with a mild, slightly sweet flavor.

Key ID: Fan/shell-shaped, white to gray, grows in shelf-like clusters on dead hardwood. Short off-center stem. Mild anise smell.

What It Looks Like

  • Cap: Fan or oyster-shell shaped, 2–8 in wide, smooth
  • Color: White, cream, gray, or tan
  • Gills: White, running down a short stubby stem

Where & When

Dead/dying hardwoods. Spring & fall primarily, but year-round in mild climates.

Confirm It's an Oyster Mushroom

Real Oyster mushrooms are thick and fleshy, growing on hardwood. If it's paper-thin and on conifer wood, it's a different species — discard it.

How to Cook

One of the most versatile wild mushrooms. Sauté, stir-fry, add to soups. Mild, slightly sweet.

Porcini (King Bolete)
Boletus edulis
EdibleJul–Oct

One of the most prized edible mushrooms in the world, known in Italian cuisine as the "king" of mushrooms. Identified by its brown cap and spongy pore surface instead of gills.

Key ID: Brown convex cap, spongy pores underneath (NOT gills), thick bulbous white stem with fine white netting. Flesh does NOT change color.

What It Looks Like

  • Cap: Smooth, convex, brown to reddish-brown, 3–10 in
  • Underside: Spongy pore surface — white young, yellow-green with age
  • Stem: Thick, bulbous, white netting on upper portion

Where & When

Under conifers and some hardwoods, in mountainous/northern forests. Summer–fall.

Confirm It's a Real Porcini

Touch a tiny piece to your tongue — a real Porcini tastes mild and nutty. If it's intensely bitter, it's a different species (not toxic, but inedible). Also check: pore surface should stay white to yellow-green, not turn pinkish.

How to Cook

One of the most sought-after mushrooms worldwide. Slice and sauté fresh, or dry for risottos and pastas.

Giant Puffball
Calvatia gigantea
EdibleAug–Oct

A large, white, ball-shaped mushroom that can grow as big as a soccer ball or larger. One of the easiest mushrooms to identify when mature.

Key ID: Round, white ball, no stem, no gills, no cap. Flesh must be solid pure white inside. Size: 4–20+ inches across.

What It Looks Like

  • Shape: Round to slightly oval ball sitting on the ground
  • Color: White outside when fresh, brownish with age
  • Inside: Must be solid, pure white, marshmallow-like flesh
  • Size: Typically 8–20 inches, sometimes larger

Where & When

Meadows, parks, open forests, lawns. Late summer–fall (Aug–Oct).

Before You Eat: Slice in Half

Inside must be solid pure white all the way through — like a marshmallow. If you see any yellow, brown, or the outline of gills forming inside, it is NOT a puffball. Discard it.

How to Cook

Slice into thick steaks, bread and fry, or sauté in butter. Mild flavor, absorbs seasonings well.

Black Trumpet
Craterellus cornucopioides
EdibleJun–Oct

A dark, funnel-shaped mushroom with a delicate, smoky flavor prized by chefs. Despite its gloomy appearance, it has no dangerous look-alikes — one of the safest wild mushrooms to forage.

Key ID: Dark gray to black funnel/trumpet shape, thin wavy edges, smooth underside (no gills). No dangerous look-alikes.

What It Looks Like

  • Shape: Thin funnel or trumpet, 1–4 inches tall
  • Color: Dark gray, brown, to black
  • Underside: Smooth to slightly wrinkled — no true gills
  • Flesh: Thin, fragile, aromatic

Where & When

Forest floors near oaks and beeches, often in mossy areas. Hard to spot due to dark color. Summer–fall.

How to Cook

Smoky, rich flavor. Excellent dried. Often called "poor man's truffle" due to its intense aroma. Sauté or add to cream sauces.

Honey Mushroom
Armillaria mellea
EdibleSep–Nov

One of the most common edible mushrooms in North American forests, growing in large clusters on dead or dying trees. Must always be cooked thoroughly — raw causes stomach upset.

Key ID: Honey-yellow to tan cap, white ring on stem, grows in large clusters on wood. White spore print. Cap has small dark scales in center.

What It Looks Like

  • Cap: 1–4 inches, honey-yellow to tan, small dark scales in center
  • Gills: White to cream, slightly attached to stem
  • Stem: Tough, fibrous, with a prominent white ring
  • Growth: Dense clusters on stumps, roots, or tree bases

Where & When

On dead/dying trees and stumps, sometimes appearing to grow from soil (actually from buried roots). Fall (Sep–Nov).

Confirm It's a Honey Mushroom

Check the spore print: it must be white (not rusty brown). The ring on the stem should stay white (not stain brown). Real Honey mushrooms grow in large dense clusters. If anything doesn't match, discard it.

How to Cook

Must cook thoroughly — raw causes stomach upset. Discard tough stems, use only caps. Mild, slightly sweet flavor.

☠️
Death Cap
Amanita phalloides
Deadly ToxicAug–Nov

Responsible for more mushroom deaths worldwide than any other species. A single mushroom can kill an adult, and symptoms don't appear until 6–24 hours after eating.

Key ID: Greenish-yellow to olive cap, white gills, skirt-like ring on stem + cup-shaped volva at base (dig it up!). Smells like honey. Half a cap can kill an adult.

What It Looks Like

  • Cap: 2–6 in, greenish-yellow to olive-green, smooth, slightly sticky
  • Gills: White, closely spaced, free (not attached to stem)
  • Stem: White, 3–6 in, with a ring (annulus) near the top
  • Base: Bulbous, sitting in a cup-like sac (volva) — often buried. DIG IT UP
  • Smell: Pleasant — honey or roses. This is deceptive.

Where & When

Under oaks and hardwoods. Parks, yards, forests. Fall/winter in mild climates, late summer–fall elsewhere.

Why It Kills

  • Contains amatoxins that destroy liver and kidneys
  • Symptoms delayed 6–24 hours — organ damage may already be happening
  • Deceptive "recovery" phase before final, potentially fatal stage
  • No reliable home antidote — ER immediately

Destroying Angel
Amanita bisporigera / A. virosa / A. ocreata
Deadly ToxicJun–Oct

One of the deadliest mushrooms in North America. Its pure white, elegant appearance makes it dangerously easy to mistake for edible species.

Key ID: Entirely pure white. White gills, fragile ring on stem, volva at base. Looks elegant and innocent. As deadly as the Death Cap.

What It Looks Like

  • Cap: 2–5 in, pure white, smooth, convex to flat
  • Gills: White, free from stem
  • Stem: White, tall, slender, fragile ring
  • Base: Bulbous with sac-like volva — DIG IT UP

The #1 Rule with White Mushrooms

If it's white, has gills, and grows from the ground — treat it as deadly until proven otherwise. Contains the same amatoxins as the Death Cap.

Jack O'Lantern
Omphalotus illudens
ToxicJul–Nov

The most common reason people accidentally eat a toxic mushroom in North America. Often confused with Chanterelles, but grows in clusters on wood and has true gills.

Key ID: Bright orange, true sharp gills, grows in clusters on/near wood. Gills glow in the dark. The #1 Chanterelle impostor.

How to Tell It's NOT a Chanterelle

Jack O'Lantern has true sharp gills (Chanterelles have blunt ridges), grows in clusters on wood (Chanterelles grow from soil), and is orange all the way through (Chanterelles are pale inside).

If Someone Eats One

Severe cramps, vomiting, diarrhea within 1–2 hours. Rarely fatal. Seek medical attention.

Fly Agaric
Amanita muscaria
ToxicJul–Nov

The iconic red-and-white "fairy tale" mushroom seen in storybooks and video games. Despite its beautiful appearance, it is toxic and causes serious symptoms if eaten.

Key ID: Bright red to orange cap with white warts/spots, white gills, white ring on stem, bulbous base with patches. Unmistakable appearance.

What It Looks Like

  • Cap: 3–10 in, bright red to orange-red, covered with white to yellowish wart-like patches
  • Gills: White, free from stem
  • Stem: White, 4–8 in, with a ring and a bulbous base with concentric scales
  • Note: Rain can wash off white spots, leaving a plain orange-red cap

Where & When

Under birch, pine, spruce, and fir trees. Found in forests and woodland edges. Summer–fall (Jul–Nov).

Toxicity

Contains ibotenic acid and muscimol. Symptoms include nausea, vomiting, confusion, hallucinations, and drowsiness. Rarely fatal in adults but dangerous. Seek medical attention if ingested.

False Morel
Gyromitra esculenta
Deadly ToxicMar–May

A wrinkled, brain-like mushroom that can be mistaken for a true Morel in spring. Contains gyromitrin, a toxic compound that can cause organ damage or death.

Key ID: Brain-like, wrinkled, lobed cap (NOT honeycomb). NOT hollow — chambered, cottony inside when sliced. Reddish-brown, irregular shape.

What It Looks Like

  • Cap: Wrinkled, brain-like lobes, reddish-brown to dark brown, irregular shape
  • Inside: Chambered, cottony — NOT hollow like a true Morel
  • Stem: Short, thick, often folded or furrowed, whitish
  • Size: 2–5 inches tall

How to Tell It's NOT a True Morel

False Morels have brain-like wrinkles (true Morels have clean honeycomb pits). Slice in half: false Morels are chambered and cottony inside, true Morels are completely hollow.

Where & When

Sandy soils near conifers, especially in spring after snowmelt. Same season as true Morels. Spring (Mar–May).

Why It's Dangerous

Contains gyromitrin, which converts to monomethylhydrazine (rocket fuel) in the body. Can cause liver failure and death. Always slice mushrooms in half to check if hollow.

If Someone Eats a Toxic Mushroom

1-800-222-1222

US Poison Control — Call immediately. Do NOT wait for symptoms.

1. Call now (symptoms can be delayed 6–24 hrs). 2. Save a sample or take photos. 3. Do NOT induce vomiting unless told to. 4. Go to the ER. 5. Note time of ingestion.

Trail Extras

Everything else you need for mushroom hunting

The Essentials

  • Small knife or pocket blade — cut mushrooms at the base cleanly
  • Paper bags or wax paper — never plastic (traps moisture)
  • Basket or mesh bag — keeps finds intact, drops spores as you walk
  • Your phone camera — photos of cap, underside, stem, base, habitat

Nice to Have

  • 10x hand lens — examine gill structure up close
  • Field notebook — tree species nearby, soil type, weather
  • Aluminum foil — wrap specimens individually
  • White + dark paper — spore prints on the trail
  • Wet wipes — always wash hands after handling unknowns
  • Where is it growing? Ground, wood, tree trunk?
  • What's nearby? Oak, beech, conifer, mixed forest?
  • How is it growing? Alone, scattered, dense cluster?
  • Cap: Color, shape, size, texture?
  • Underside: Gills, pores, ridges, or teeth?
  • Stem: Color, thickness, ring/skirt, cup at base?
  • Cut it open: Hollow? Solid? Color change?
  • Smell: Fruity? Earthy? Unpleasant?
  • Photos taken? Top, underside, stem, base, habitat — at least 5

Chanterelle (Rebozuelo)
Cantharellus cibarius
ComestibleJun–Oct

Uno de los hongos silvestres comestibles más populares del mundo, apreciado por su color dorado, aroma afrutado y sabor ligeramente picante.

Clave: Embudo dorado, falsas láminas (pliegues bifurcados), olor afrutado a albaricoque. Crece del suelo, nunca de madera.

Cómo Se Ve

  • Sombrero: Dorado a amarillo yema, 5–12 cm, ondulado tipo embudo
  • Parte inferior: Falsas láminas — pliegues que bajan por el tallo
  • Tallo: Sólido, mismo color dorado
  • Olor: Afrutado, como albaricoque

Dónde y Cuándo

Pisos de bosque cerca de robles y coníferas. Verano a principios de otoño.

Confirma que es un Chanterelle Real

Debe tener pliegues romos bifurcados (no láminas filosas), crecer del suelo (no en racimos sobre madera), y la carne por dentro debe ser pálida/blanca (no naranja por completo).

Cómo Cocinarlo

Cocina bien en mantequilla. Nunca crudo. Uno de los mejores hongos silvestres del mundo.

Morilla (Morel)
Morchella esculenta
ComestibleMar–May

Un hongo de primavera muy buscado con un sombrero tipo panal inconfundible. Entre los hongos silvestres más reconocibles y favoritos de los recolectores.

Clave: Sombrero tipo panal con hoyos profundos. Completamente hueco al cortarlo. Forma de cono, canela a marrón oscuro.

Cómo Se Ve

  • Sombrero: Superficie de panal con hoyos y crestas
  • Interior: Completamente hueco — la clave #1
  • Tallo: Blanco a crema, también hueco

Dónde y Cuándo

Cerca de olmos y fresnos muertos. Áreas post-incendio. Primavera (Mar–May).

Confirma que es una Morilla Real

Córtala a la mitad: debe ser completamente hueca por dentro. Si tiene cámaras o es algodonosa, no es una Morilla — descártala. El sombrero debe tener hoyos tipo panal limpios, no arrugas tipo cerebro.

Cómo Cocinarlo

Remoja en agua con sal 30 min. Cocina completamente. Un manjar.

Bejín Gigante
Calvatia gigantea
ComestibleAgo–Oct

Un hongo grande, blanco y redondo como un balón de fútbol o más grande. Uno de los hongos más fáciles de identificar cuando es maduro.

Clave: Bola redonda y blanca, sin tallo, sin láminas, sin sombrero. Carne blanca pura y sólida por dentro. Tamaño: 10–50+ cm.

Cómo Se Ve

  • Forma: Bola redonda a ligeramente ovalada sobre el suelo
  • Color: Blanco por fuera cuando fresco, se oscurece con la edad
  • Interior: Debe ser blanco sólido y puro, como malvavisco

Dónde y Cuándo

Praderas, parques, bosques abiertos, céspedes. Final de verano a otoño (Ago–Oct).

Debe ser blanco sólido y puro por completo — como un malvavisco. Si ves amarillo, marrón, o el contorno de láminas formándose adentro, NO es un bejín. Descártalo.

Cómo Cocinarlo

Corta en "filetes" gruesos, empaniza y fríe, o saltea en mantequilla. Sabor suave, absorbe bien los condimentos.

Hongo de Miel
Armillaria mellea
ComestibleSep–Nov

Uno de los hongos comestibles más comunes en bosques de Norteamérica, creciendo en racimos grandes sobre árboles muertos. Siempre se debe cocinar — crudo causa malestar.

Clave: Sombrero amarillo miel a canela, anillo blanco en el tallo, crece en racimos grandes sobre madera. Esporas blancas. Escamas oscuras en el centro del sombrero.

Cómo Se Ve

  • Sombrero: 3–10 cm, amarillo miel a canela, escamas oscuras en el centro
  • Láminas: Blancas a crema, ligeramente adheridas al tallo
  • Tallo: Duro, fibroso, con anillo blanco prominente
  • Crecimiento: Racimos densos en tocones, raíces o base de árboles

Dónde y Cuándo

En árboles muertos y tocones. A veces parece crecer del suelo (de raíces enterradas). Otoño (Sep–Nov).

Confirma que es un Hongo de Miel

Verifica la huella de esporas: debe ser blanca (no marrón óxido). El anillo en el tallo debe quedarse blanco (no teñirse de marrón). Crece en racimos grandes y densos. Si algo no coincide, descártalo.

Cómo Cocinarlo

Debe cocinarse bien — crudo causa malestar estomacal. Descarta los tallos duros, usa solo los sombreros.

Hongo de la Muerte (Death Cap)
Amanita phalloides
MortalAgo–Nov

Responsable de más muertes por hongos en el mundo que cualquier otra especie. Un solo hongo puede matar a un adulto, y los síntomas no aparecen hasta 6–24 horas después.

Clave: Sombrero verde-amarillento a oliva, láminas blancas, anillo tipo falda + volva tipo copa en la base. Huele a miel. Media cápsula mata a un adulto.

Cómo Se Ve

  • Sombrero: 5–15 cm, verde-amarillento a oliva, liso
  • Láminas: Blancas, muy juntas, libres del tallo
  • Tallo: Blanco, 8–15 cm, con anillo
  • Base: Bulbosa con volva — DESENTIERRA LA BASE

Por Qué Mata

  • Amatoxinas que destruyen hígado y riñones
  • Síntomas se retrasan 6–24 horas
  • Fase engañosa de "mejoría" antes de la etapa final
  • Sin antídoto casero — urgencias inmediatamente

Ángel Destructor
Amanita bisporigera / A. virosa / A. ocreata
MortalJun–Oct

Uno de los hongos más mortales de Norteamérica. Su apariencia blanca y elegante lo hace peligrosamente fácil de confundir con especies comestibles.

Clave: Todo blanco puro. Láminas blancas, anillo frágil, volva en la base. Tan letal como el Hongo de la Muerte.

Cómo Se Ve

  • Sombrero blanco puro, 5–12 cm, liso
  • Láminas blancas, libres del tallo
  • Tallo blanco, alto, anillo frágil
  • Base bulbosa con volva — DESENTIERRA

Regla #1 con Hongos Blancos

Si es blanco, tiene láminas y crece del suelo — trátalo como mortal.

Jack O'Lantern (Hongo Linterna)
Omphalotus illudens
TóxicoJul–Nov

La razón más común por la que la gente come accidentalmente un hongo tóxico en Norteamérica. Se confunde con Chanterelles, pero crece en racimos sobre madera.

Clave: Naranja brillante, láminas verdaderas y filosas, crece en racimos sobre madera. El impostor #1 del Chanterelle.

Cómo Saber que NO es un Chanterelle

El Jack O'Lantern tiene láminas verdaderas y filosas (los Chanterelles tienen pliegues romos), crece en racimos sobre madera (los Chanterelles crecen del suelo), y es naranja por completo por dentro (los Chanterelles son pálidos).

Si Alguien Come Uno

Calambres, vómito, diarrea en 1–2 horas. Rara vez fatal. Busca atención médica.

Matamoscas (Fly Agaric)
Amanita muscaria
TóxicoJul–Nov

El icónico hongo rojo con puntos blancos de los cuentos de hadas y videojuegos. A pesar de su belleza, es tóxico y causa síntomas serios si se come.

Clave: Sombrero rojo brillante a naranja con verrugas/puntos blancos, láminas blancas, anillo en el tallo, base bulbosa con parches. Apariencia inconfundible.

Cómo Se Ve

  • Sombrero: 8–25 cm, rojo brillante a naranja-rojo, cubierto de verrugas blancas a amarillentas
  • Láminas: Blancas, libres del tallo
  • Tallo: Blanco, 10–20 cm, con anillo y base bulbosa con escamas
  • Nota: La lluvia puede lavar los puntos blancos, dejando un sombrero naranja-rojo liso

Dónde y Cuándo

Bajo abedules, pinos, abetos. En bosques y bordes de arboledas. Verano a otoño (Jul–Nov).

Toxicidad

Contiene ácido iboténico y muscimol. Síntomas: náuseas, vómitos, confusión, alucinaciones, somnolencia. Rara vez fatal en adultos pero peligroso. Busca atención médica si se ingiere.

Falsa Morilla
Gyromitra esculenta
MortalMar–May

Un hongo arrugado tipo cerebro que puede confundirse con la Morilla verdadera en primavera. Contiene giromitrín, una toxina que puede causar daño a órganos o la muerte.

Clave: Sombrero tipo cerebro, arrugado y lobulado (NO panal). NO hueco — por dentro tiene carne algodonosa dividida en cámaras. Marrón rojizo, forma irregular.

Cómo Se Ve

  • Sombrero: Lóbulos arrugados tipo cerebro, marrón rojizo a oscuro, forma irregular
  • Interior: Con cámaras, algodonoso — NO hueco como una Morilla verdadera
  • Tallo: Corto, grueso, arrugado, blanquecino

Cómo Saber que NO es una Morilla Verdadera

La Falsa Morilla tiene arrugas tipo cerebro (las verdaderas tienen hoyos tipo panal). Córtala a la mitad: la falsa es algodonosa con cámaras por dentro, la verdadera es completamente hueca.

Dónde y Cuándo

Suelos arenosos cerca de coníferas, especialmente en primavera. Misma temporada que las Morillas verdaderas. Primavera (Mar–May).

Por Qué Es Peligroso

Contiene giromitrín, que se convierte en monometilhidrazina en el cuerpo. Puede causar fallo hepático y muerte. Siempre corta los hongos a la mitad para verificar si son huecos.

Extras para el Sendero

Todo lo que necesitas para buscar hongos

Lo Esencial

  • Cuchillo pequeño — corta hongos limpiamente en la base
  • Bolsas de papel — nunca plástico
  • Canasta o bolsa de malla — mantiene los hallazgos intactos
  • Tu teléfono (cámara) — fotos del sombrero, abajo, tallo, base

Bueno Tener

  • Lupa 10x — examina láminas y poros de cerca
  • Libreta de campo — árboles cercanos, tipo de suelo
  • Papel aluminio — envuelve ejemplares
  • Papel blanco + oscuro — para huellas de esporas
  • ¿Dónde crece? Suelo, madera, tronco?
  • ¿Qué hay cerca? Roble, haya, conífera?
  • ¿Cómo crece? Solo, disperso, racimo?
  • Sombrero: Color, forma, tamaño, textura?
  • Parte inferior: Láminas, poros, pliegues, espinas?
  • Tallo: Color, grosor, anillo, copa en la base?
  • Córtalo: Hueco? Sólido? Cambia de color?
  • Olor: Afrutado? Terroso? Desagradable?
  • ¿Fotos tomadas? Al menos 5 ángulos
